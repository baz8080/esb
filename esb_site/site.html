Nationally this month

This site snapshots ESB Networks' public PowerCheck feed and keeps the results. ESB publishes no historical archive and purges each outage from the feed a few hours after restoration, so nothing before exists here, and days before it are shown as “no data” rather than as no outages. Storm days are not excluded here, though ESB excludes them from its own published figures, so winter months will read worse than ESB's.

What this measures

The grade is ESB's own published aim: supply restored within 4 hours in 95% of cases. Each county is scored on the share of its fault-interrupted customers restored within that window - A meets the 95% aim, then B 90%, C 80%, D 70%, F below. Planned works are excluded.

How the grade is worked out

The standard comes from the Customer Charter the CRU approves, under the Restore Supply Guarantee: our aim is to restore supply within less than 4 hours in 95% of cases. The same guarantee pays compensation if you are without power for 24 hours after ESB was made aware, and again for each further 12 hours; outages past that mark are counted separately on each county page.

A month with fewer than five faults is left ungraded, because one long outage in a quiet county would swing the letter two bands. Planned works are excluded exactly as the CRU's incentive excludes them: they are notified in advance and never report a restore time (they simply stop being listed), so their true duration is not something this data knows.

The other figure: Customer Minutes Lost

CML is the minutes an average customer spends without supply over a year, and it is the unit the CRU regulates ESB Networks in. For 2024 the CRU target was 78.7 and ESB Networks reported 117.47 (Distribution Annual Performance Report 2024; targets are set under the CRU's PR5 framework).

It is shown here for comparison but does not set the grade, because this data cannot reproduce it on ESB's scale. The durations match almost exactly, an implied – minutes per interrupted customer against ESB's –, but the feed counts about – more interrupted customers, since it reports everyone on the affected section when a fault is logged while ESB settles on a smaller number once crews have isolated it. A share of customers is immune to that; a total of them is not.

How to read an outage

Each outage shows when it began and when supply was restored, both times as ESB reports them. The rows between are the customer count falling as crews restore sections, which is the one thing no ESB field records and has to come from watching the feed.

ESB back-dates the start to when the fault began rather than when it was published: across 1,460 outages the field was revised 8 times, and not one outage appeared in the feed before its own reported start. So the durations here are the outage, not the notice.

This site polls every 30 minutes, so a short outage can begin and end between two polls and be recorded only after it finished. That affects when we noticed an outage, not the times shown, which are ESB's own. Where ESB never published a restore time the end is marked as an estimate or as the last sighting, and never presented as a measurement.

ESB opens a new record each time a fault's scope changes, so records sharing a location and start time are folded into one outage here. A fault that returns to the same spot within 15 minutes is a separate interruption and stays a separate row, tagged as a repeat.

What it cannot tell you

ESB excludes storm days from its published figures so they compare with other utilities: To benchmark our outage performance against other utilities storm days (the effects of severe weather) are removed for unplanned CI and CML reporting (DAPR 2024). The Customer Charter likewise exempts exceptional cases such as storms from the restoration guarantee. Nothing in the public feed identifies a storm day, so this site excludes nothing.

Counties come from the nearest Census Small Area to each outage's coordinates, because the feed carries no county field. A "customer" in ESB's figures is a meter (a home, farm or business), and ESB publishes no per-county count, so the county figures here are its roughly 2.5 million meters split by each county's share of the Census 2022 population.
